# Interview guide

Use this to walk the referee through what happened before anything is drafted. The goal is a complete, first-hand set of facts. Most weak reports are weak because something was never asked, not because it was written badly.

## How to run it

- One incident at a time, in the order they happened.
- Ask 3 to 5 questions per message. Never dump the whole list.
- Start open ("Walk me through what happened in the 38th minute"), then use the checklists below to fill gaps.
- Ask about what they saw and heard, not what they concluded. "What did she say?" beats "Was it dissent?"
- When an answer is a judgment ("it was dangerous", "he was irate"), ask for the observable facts behind it: what body part, how fast, what words, how loud, how close.
- When an answer came from someone else (an AR, a coach, a player), note who, and ask whether the referee saw any of it.
- If they don't know or don't remember, record that. Never fill a gap with a guess.
- Before drafting, play back a short bullet summary of the facts per incident and ask: "Anything wrong or missing? Anything here you didn't see or hear yourself?"

## Match details (ask once)

- Date, kickoff time, venue and field
- Competition or league, age group, game number
- Full team names as registered, home and away, final score
- Referee name. Referee ID or license number only if the league's form asks for it.
- Assistant referees and fourth official: names and positions (AR1/AR2)
- Half length; how long each half actually ran; whether minutes come from a timing app with a running clock (for example RefSix) and where its second-half clock started
- A screenshot of the timing app's event log, if they have one. Check it for cards logged to the wrong team or not removed after being rescinded.

## Every incident

- Minute (as logged) and where on the field (third, side, inside or outside the penalty area)
- Who: number, name, team. For team officials: name, role, team.
- What they did, step by step. What they said, word for word, profanity included.
- Who saw it: the referee, an AR, the fourth official. Where the referee was, how far away, and whether the view was clear or blocked.
- What the referee did: whistle, advantage, warning, card, the misconduct reason given
- Was the ball in or out of play? What came first if more than one thing happened before the whistle?
- Restart: type and location
- Aftermath: how the player or official left, any reaction, any injury and treatment
- Any other official who should write their own supplemental report

## Fouls leading to a caution or send-off

- Point of contact and body part used (studs, sole, knee, elbow, hands)
- Speed and force; from which direction (front, side, behind)
- Was the ball playable? How far away was it? Was there an attempt to play it?
- Feet off the ground, leg raised, studs showing?
- Result for the opponent: fell, needed treatment, left the game
- Which category the referee applied: careless, reckless, or excessive force (serious foul play or violent conduct)

## DOGSO or SPA

- Distance from the offense to goal
- General direction of play
- Likelihood of keeping or gaining control of the ball
- Location and number of defenders
- In the penalty area: was it an attempt to play the ball? (affects caution vs send-off)

## Second caution

- Get the full facts for both cautions, not just the second one.

## Offensive, insulting, or abusive language or gestures

- Exact words or a description of the gesture
- Who it was directed at
- Who could hear or see it (players, spectators, officials)

## Dissent (players or team officials)

- Exact words and actions
- Directed at whom
- How loud; how many people could hear
- Any earlier warnings, and what was said when warning
- What happened after the warning

These questions inform the referee's decision. The report itself states the words and actions, not an analysis of them.

## Team officials

- Name, role, team, and where they were (technical area or elsewhere)
- Warnings given before any card, with the words used
- Exact words and actions leading to each card
- If dismissed: did they leave, how long it took, where they went, any communication with the team after
- Who supervised the team afterward. Was there another carded team official present?
- Entering the field: was permission asked or given? Why did they enter? What happened next?

## Injuries

- Head injury or not. If a head injury was suspected, what protocol was followed.
- Did anyone enter the field, with or without permission?
- Did the player leave the field, and for how long? Did they return? When?

## Changed or rescinded decisions

- What the original decision was
- What prompted the change, and who asked
- Exactly when it was changed: before play restarted, before the match was abandoned
- What fact made the referee change it

## Spectators

- Which team they appeared to support, and how the referee knew
- What they said or did, word for word
- Who the referee asked to deal with it (a coach, a club official) and what happened

## Referee assault or abuse

- Any physical contact: what, where on the body, with what force
- Threats: exact words
- Witnesses, including other officials
- Whether the referee is safe and whether police or medical help was involved
- Remind them this follows a separate, faster reporting path (often 24 to 48 hours, directly to the state association or sanctioning body) and to contact their assignor today

## Abandonment or termination

- Time, score, and reason
- Steps taken before abandoning (warnings, requests to coaches, delays)
- Who was told and how

## Other unusual events

- Weather or lightning delays (times out and back), field or equipment problems, roster or pass issues, late arrival, forfeit
